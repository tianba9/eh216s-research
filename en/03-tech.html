These Numbers Hold Up: Parameter Credibility Under Three Checks

Manufacturers' figures often cannot be verified directly — there is no wind-tunnel data, no empty weight, no rotor diameter. They can still be checked: whether time closes, whether the power magnitude is reasonable, and whether the aircraft is abnormal when set beside its peers. If all three checks pass, the figures can be accepted.

01Three Verification Methods

With key data missing, feasibility judgments rest not on trust but on cross-checking. This report uses three mutually independent paths of verification.

Table 3-1　Three verification paths and their logic
MethodWhat it testsFailure signal
Time closureWhether range, endurance and cruise speed can hold simultaneouslyTime needed to cover the published range at the published cruise speed significantly exceeds the published endurance
Order-of-magnitude checkWhether power, energy and disc loading fall within the physical range of same-class aircraftPower density beyond current motor levels, or energy demand beyond the 620 kg carryable limit
BenchmarkingWhether the performance distribution relative to similar products is abnormalAt equal configuration and seating class, one metric deviates markedly from the group

02Time-Closure Check

This is the cheapest and most effective check. Published values: range 30 km, normal level flight 90 km/h, maximum endurance 25 min. Cruising at 90 km/h, 30 km takes 20 minutes, leaving 5 minutes to cover climb, descent, landing and reserve.

The significance of closure lies in “consistency”. If a manufacturer inflated three numbers separately, they would almost never fit exactly into a complete mission profile. The three figures fit seamlessly, indicating that they record a real flight-test profile.

A corollary obtained along the way

Average ground speed is only 72 km/h (30 km / 25 min), far below the 90 km/h level-flight speed. The entire difference is consumed by vertical take-off and landing and by acceleration/deceleration segments — for a multirotor the vertical phases are not only slow but also the most power-hungry. This explains why shortening the range cannot linearly extend endurance, and why “battery swapping” is closer to real operational needs than “fast charging”.

The gap that must be acknowledged after benchmarking

In speed and range, the EH216-S trails same-class aircraft by an order of magnitude: 30 km against 161 km, 130 km/h against 322 km/h. This is not a product defect but the price of the configuration — a multirotor has no wing to provide lift. What it gains in return is a two-to-three-year lead in certification and an acceptable cost structure in two-seat, short-haul, low-frequency scenarios. It must be judged with the yardstick of “certification leadership”, not “performance leadership”.

Overall judgment: the aircraft itself has reached an operable state, while the operating system is not yet mature. This distinction matters — it means the main risks over the next three years lie not in the product but in airspace, vertiports and operating rules.

05Where the Bottleneck Really Is

A common claim is that “endurance is inadequate”. This judgment is too coarse to guide action. Ranked by the hardness of the constraints, there are three layers of real bottleneck.

Table 3-4　Three layers of bottleneck and their relief conditions
LayerBottleneckQuantitative manifestationRelief condition
Layer 1
Energy		Battery system specific energy about 200 Wh/kg200 kg of batteries supports only 40 kWh and 25 minRaise specific energy to 350–400 Wh/kg, or adopt hybrid range extension
Layer 2
Aerodynamics		No wing surface; lift is produced by rotors throughoutCruise power is about 80% of hover, with no minimum-power cruise stateChange configuration (compound wing / tilt-rotor), at the cost of restarting certification
Layer 3
Operations		Recharge time 120 min, far longer than the 25 min flightDuty cycle about 1 : 4.8, locking daily utilisation per aircraftBattery swapping, fast-charging capability, or fleet-scale dilution

The third layer is worth noting. It is neither a materials nor an aerodynamics problem, yet it does the most damage to commercial viability — because the aircraft can wait for batteries, but operations cannot. Chapter 05 will turn it into a specific cost figure.

Judgment on “changing configuration”

EHang released the VT35 in October 2025 with a design range of about 200 km, a direct response to the second-layer bottleneck. But changing configuration means going through the certification process again; previously accumulated certification evidence cannot be transferred directly. It is the right path, but one that will take several more years.

06If Verification Were Possible: A Flight-Test Outline

This report could not carry out measurements, but it can offer a verification plan. The five items below form a minimally credible validation plan for testing the report's key inferences.

V1 to V4 need only a single site visit; V5 requires rig facilities. If all five pass, the conclusions of Chapter 03 and Chapter 05 are confirmed; if any deviates, the corresponding conclusion must be revised against the measured value.

07Chapter Verdict

Verdict 1

Technical feasibility holds, but only within the defined scenario

The figures are self-consistent, the magnitudes reasonable, and the aircraft is in series production and operation. As a “10–30 km short-haul passenger aircraft” it is technically feasible; as an “urban commuter aircraft” it is not.

Verdict 2

The lead comes from certification, not performance

Compared with Joby and Archer, it trails by an order of magnitude in speed and range but leads certification by two to three years. Judging it by a performance yardstick inevitably yields the wrong conclusion.

Verdict 3

The hardest technical bottleneck is not in the air but on the ground

The low duty cycle caused by recharge time compresses commercial space more directly than battery specific energy. This is the central thread of Chapter 05.

08Chapter Summary

The three checks are independent: time closure asks whether the three figures come from the same mission profile, the magnitude check whether power and energy fall within physical limits, and benchmarking whether anything is abnormal relative to same-class aircraft. All three pass — this shows only “credibility”, not “leadership”.

Compared with Joby and Archer, speed and range are an order of magnitude behind; the EH216-S leads in certification progress. The bottleneck has three layers, and the hardest is not in the air: recharge time locks daily utilisation per aircraft.
