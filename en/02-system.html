Configuration Is Destiny

Why not a compound wing or a tilt-rotor? The answer lies less in aerodynamics than in certification and redundancy. This chapter decomposes the EH216-S to component level and shows how its layout grants fault tolerance while fixing its range ceiling.

01Four-level decomposition

The aircraft sits inside a mission, a system-of-systems and a set of components. Its certification boundary is unusual: the aircraft, the ground control station and the data link form one certificated system.

02Configuration analysis

Eight arms carry coaxial counter-rotating pairs: no tail rotor, no control surfaces. That simplicity shortens certification and multiplies redundancy, at the cost of cruise efficiency.

Table 2-1　Gains and costs
AspectDirectionMechanism
Fault toleranceGainAny one of 16 rotors can fail with torque compensation from the opposite side
CertificationGainNo tilting mechanism or variable pitch; failure modes are enumerable

Thirty kilometres is not an engineering shortfall but a mathematical consequence of the configuration.

04Power and energy

With a 620 kg mass and 16 rotors, each rotor produces about 380 N in hover. Assuming a 1.2 m diameter, total disc area is 18.1 m² and ideal induced power is 71.2 kW; with a figure of merit of 0.70 and 92% electrical efficiency, hover demand is about 113 kW.

The mass budget implies 200 kg × 200 Wh/kg = 40 kWh; the power integral gives 40.8 kWh. Two independent paths converge, so the published figures are not exaggerated.

06Chapter Summary

At component level the trade-off becomes concrete: 8 arms and 16 rotors buy fault tolerance and a shorter approval path, at the price of cruise efficiency and range. The mass budget gives a 24.2% payload fraction; the power integral gives 40.8 kWh, consistent with the mass-derived 40 kWh.

Thirty kilometres is a structural limit of a multi-rotor at 620 kg with current batteries.
